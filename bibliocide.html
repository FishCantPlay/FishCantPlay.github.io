A TALE THAT REFUSES TO END

Bibliocide

✦ ❧ ✦

Every story has an ending. What happens when someone refuses to let it happen?

A first-person puzzle game about twisted fairytales, escapism and the uncomfortable point where fiction stops being a safe place to hide.

CHAPTER I

The Story

❧ ✦ ❧

Once upon a time...

Bibliocide is a first-person puzzle game about escaping escapism. It opens with the player finding a rat clawing at a locked door. After picking it up and entering, they are pulled into a warped fairytale world where familiar stories have become increasingly unsettling.

With the rat as a companion, the player works through the fairytale rooms, solving puzzles and collecting books. The journey ends at the furnace, where the player has to choose between returning to the comfort of fantasy or rejecting it and facing reality.

The game is not really about saving a world. It is about recognising when something that once felt comforting has become a way of avoiding reality, and how difficult it can be to let that go.

CHAPTER II

A World Between the Pages

✦ ❧ ✦

The game moves through a series of fairytale rooms. The plan changed during development, so this is the final intended flow rather than every idea making it into the build.

CINDERELLA'S ROOMInsert a screenshot of the room or its puzzle.

Cinderella's Room

The player gathers five items for Cinderella and places them on the central table. Once the set is complete, an unexpected sixth slot appears and Cinderella demands one of her sister's eyes. The rat was also intended to retrieve an item from a cupboard that the player could not reach.

JACK'S ROOMInsert a screenshot of the beanstalk, goose or Bean Seller.

Jack's Room

The Bean Seller trades fertiliser for coins, which the rat helps retrieve through mouseholes. The player uses the fertiliser to grow the beanstalk in stages, then buys a special bean to scare the goose away from the book it guards. The original idea was more mechanically layered, but was simplified during development.

PLANNED ALICE ROOMInsert the concept art if you want to show the cut room.

The Alice Room — Cut

This planned room was based on Alice in Wonderland. The player would shrink after eating a cake, avoid a hostile White Rabbit and use the rat's squeaks as an audio cue while navigating a stealth section. It was cut because of time and technical limitations, so it did not make it into the final game.

THE FURNACE ROOMInsert the final room screenshot or ending image.

The Furnace Room

The final room presents the player with the books they have collected. The Narrator tries to persuade them to sit down, read and return to the stories. The player can remain in the chair and surrender to escapism, or burn the books and leave the fairytale world behind. If they burn the books, the rat jumps into the fire and the player returns to reality alone.

CHAPTER III

My Part in the Story

❧ ✦ ❧

More than just the art

My role ended up being a lot broader than I originally expected. I came into the project wanting to focus mainly on 2D character work and voice acting, but I gradually became the project lead and took responsibility for a lot of the organisation, documentation and creative direction as well.

I wrote the X-Statement, Aesthetic and Game Concept sections of the Group Design Document, helped shape the game's tone and narrative direction, and structured most of the pitch presentation. I also set up the Trello board, assigned responsibilities, maintained the priority task list and tried to keep communication and production moving.

Creatively, I produced character concepts, finished illustrations, rat animations, environmental concepts, a visual style mockup, voice acting and the main menu music. I also helped refine scripts, puzzle flow and playtest feedback. The sections below separate the work into galleries so the page doesn't become one massive wall of images.

CHAPTER V

Voice Acting & Music

❧ ✦ ❧

I recorded the dialogue and composed the music in BandLab. The players below are ready for the exported audio files; I just need to add each file to the audio folder and update its path.

Voice Acting — Cinderella

I recorded all of Cinderella's dialogue using my Blue Yeti microphone and edited the clips in BandLab. I recorded several takes with different deliveries, ranging from warm and gentle to more detached, so the team could choose what suited the character. I used noise gates to reduce background noise, although I didn't have a soundproof recording space.

Add the exported Cinderella dialogue here: audio/cinderella-dialogue.mp3

Voice Acting — The Narrator

I wrote and performed the Narrator's final monologue. I wanted the speech to feel coaxing and comforting on the surface, but increasingly manipulative. The monologue was designed to be interrupted by the player's interaction with the chair, so the player could physically interrupt the attempt to pull them back into escapism.

Add the exported Narrator monologue here: audio/narrator-monologue.mp3

Main Menu Music

I composed the main menu track in BandLab using my voice because I didn't have the cable needed to connect my keyboard to my PC. I layered a low humming loop with higher harmonies, then added free-use sounds of a fireplace, turning pages and a book being placed down. The humming developed a violin-like quality, which I kept because it suited the mournful fairytale atmosphere.

Add the exported main menu track here: audio/main-menu-theme.mp3

Experimental Ending Track — Unfinished

I also started a second piece for the ending where the player stays in the chair. I used soft layered vocal tones and a high-pitched frequency that gradually intensified, intended to suggest dissociation and the slow loss of awareness. I never finished this track, so this is an optional space for a work-in-progress excerpt rather than a finished game asset.

CHAPTER VI

How I Worked

✦ ❧ ✦

01. Planning

I used Clip Studio Paint for the 2D work and Trello to organise tasks, assets and priorities. I also wrote key sections of the GDD and helped define the game's direction.

02. Creating

I made character concepts, finished illustrations, room layouts and animation cycles. I used visual references and simplified details where they better suited the game's style.

I recorded dialogue and composed the menu music in BandLab, working around the equipment I had available and editing the audio for use in the game.

04. Testing

I helped organise playtesting, contributed to the feedback form, looked for sequence breaks and considered how player feedback related to the intended experience.

CHAPTER VII

Project Organisation & Teamwork

❧ ✦ ❧

Keeping the project moving

I became project lead because the group needed someone to give the work some structure. I set up the Trello board, divided responsibilities around the strengths people said they had, maintained the priority task list and attended all but one of our Tuesday Scrum meetings. I also wrote the X-Statement, Aesthetic and Game Concept sections of the GDD, and took primary responsibility for restructuring the pitch presentation after the shared slides became unfocused.

In practice, I spent a lot of time trying to keep everyone working from the same plan. We had recurring communication problems, tasks were not always updated, and some decisions were made in private conversations rather than in the group channels. I repeatedly had to clarify what had been agreed and bring the discussion back to the shared project.

One of the biggest problems was the gap between the assets being made and the assets being implemented. At the greybox playtest, my rat sprites, animations, Cinderella artwork, voice lines and main menu music were not in the build, despite having been submitted. The second playtest was much closer to what we had planned, with most of these assets finally included. There were still implementation issues, including the rat run animation moving backwards instead of flipping direction and some prepared incorrect-item dialogue not being used.

I also helped with script revisions, clarified puzzle flow, posed for reference photographs and supported the playtest process. I tried to keep the project realistic when scope became too large, including supporting a consistent rat interaction mechanic rather than introducing a new ability in every room. The Alice room was ultimately cut because of time and technical limitations.

This role taught me that having a plan is only one part of project management. People need to communicate, update their progress and take responsibility for their part of the work. I was able to keep producing my own assets, but the amount of time spent organising and correcting problems reduced the time I could spend on the creative work I originally wanted to focus on.

CHAPTER VIII

Playtesting & Evaluation

✦ ❧ ✦

From greybox to a fuller game

The first greybox test showed that the core layout and concept were understandable, and players responded well to the idea, level design and dialogue. Some people found the subtitles too fast to read, which reinforced why I wanted the voice acting included. The test also exposed that many of our completed assets had not yet been integrated.

By the second playtest, the game felt much more complete. The artwork, rat animations, voice acting and main menu music were present. Players responded positively to the atmosphere, the spoken dialogue and the rat's personality. Some technical issues remained, including the rat moving backwards during its run animation and bugs that could interrupt progression.

I was pleased that players enjoyed the game and that the art and audio were noticed. At the same time, I could see how much stronger the final experience could have been with better communication and a more reliable implementation process.

CHAPTER IX

What I Learned

❧ ✦ ❧

I'm proud of the amount and range of work I produced for Bibliocide. I came into the project mainly wanting to practise 2D character work and voice acting, but ended up contributing to visual design, animation, audio, documentation, level concepts, playtesting and project organisation. The positive feedback on the rat animation, the atmosphere and the voice acting reassured me that the creative decisions I made were working.

The project also made me realise how much I value structure, transparency and shared responsibility. I found it difficult when assets were not implemented or decisions were made without checking with the rest of the team. A lot of my time went into keeping the project organised instead of developing my own work, and that became tiring.

Looking back, my direct communication style helped me be clear, but it could also make disagreements more tense. In future projects, I want to be more deliberate about how I lead: keeping the same standards and clarity, but approaching difficult conversations in a way that is less defensive and more constructive. I also want to establish responsibilities and communication expectations earlier, rather than repeatedly trying to fix problems once they have already affected production.

Even with the issues, I'm glad we made something that players enjoyed. Bibliocide may not have reached the version I originally imagined, but I learned a lot about adapting creative work to technical limits, explaining visual ideas, managing a team and seeing a project through when things become difficult. That's what I'll take forward into my next projects.
